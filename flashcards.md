<h3>The three-face card: question, hint, answer</h3>

<p>Most flashcards are two-sided: question on the front, answer on the back, and a very short trip between them. Arc's cards have three faces, and the middle one changes how studying feels.</p>

<p>You see the question. Try to answer it from memory. Not quite there? Press <strong>H</strong> and a hint appears — not the answer, a nudge toward it. That moment of straining toward the answer with a little help is exactly where memory gets made. From the hint face, flipping takes you to the full answer. If you knew it cold, skip the hint entirely and flip straight through.</p>

<p>The face cycles are simple: question → answer → question, or question → hint → answer. Nothing to configure. It's the difference between recognition ("oh yeah, I knew that") and recall ("give me a second… the three tiers are…"). Recall is the part that survives until exam day.</p>

<div class="section-header">
  <span class="section-tag">Arc vs Anki</span>
  <h2>Arc vs Anki: generation versus scheduling.</h2>
</div>

<p>Anki is the elephant in every flashcard conversation, so let's be direct about it. Anki is excellent software with a fifteen-year track record, and its spaced-repetition scheduling is still the gold standard for moving knowledge into long-term memory. If your problem is "I have good cards and need to know exactly when to see each one again," Anki is a great answer.</p>

<p>But look at where Anki's effort goes. The decks. Somebody — usually you, at 11pm, with a textbook open — writes every card by hand. Arc attacks that bottleneck instead: point it at any content on screen and get a studyable deck in seconds. The comparison is really about two different jobs:</p>

<table class="comparison-table">
  <tr><th></th><th>Arc</th><th>Anki</th></tr>
  <tr><td>Card creation</td><td>✅ Automatic from any on-screen content</td><td>❌ Written by hand (or imported)</td></tr>
  <tr><td>PDF to flashcards</td><td>✅ One action, no upload</td><td>⚠️ Via import tools and add-ons</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Hints on cards</td><td>✅ Built into every card</td><td>⚠️ Possible, but manual per card</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Spaced repetition</td><td>⚠️ Simple reviewed counters</td><td>✅ The gold standard (FSRS)</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Setup effort</td><td>✅ None — it works on your screen</td><td>⚠️ Learning curve, deck config</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Price</td><td>✅ Free tier included</td><td>✅ Free (iOS app paid)</td></tr>
</table>

<p>Seen that way, they're not really competitors — they cover different halves of the studying problem. Arc is the generation engine: zero-effort decks from whatever you're already reading, with a hint layer most hand-written decks never get. Anki is the scheduling engine: long-term retention for material you've committed to for months or years.</p>

<p>Plenty of students run both. Arc to turn this week's lecture PDFs into decks tonight; Anki for the boards or finals material that needs spaced repetition across six months. And if you've bounced off Anki before — the deck options, the add-ons, the import formats — Arc is the low-friction way to get actual recall practice going this week, with nothing to configure.</p>

<p>One honest limitation: Arc's Reviewed counter is a tally, not a scheduling algorithm. It tells you which decks you've neglected; it won't compute the optimal interval for card 47. If long-term spaced repetition is your core need, that's a reason to keep Anki in your stack. Arc's bet is that the bottleneck for most people isn't scheduling — it's that they never have enough cards to schedule.</p>
